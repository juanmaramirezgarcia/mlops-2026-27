CI, CD and CT, when the product is a model

minutes 8–10
Point
each letter grows when the product is a model.
Say it
CI (continuous integration): every change is checked automatically; for ML that means code and data. CD (continuous delivery): a checked change can reach production automatically; for ML what is delivered is a pipeline that deploys the prediction service. CT (continuous training): new, and only in ML: retrain and serve the model automatically when new data arrives or performance drops. Analogy for CT: a bakery that bakes fresh bread every morning with today's flour, instead of selling last month's loaf. Callback: the demo repository has CI and the start of CD; CT would be a schedule that runs it on new data.
Land
"CI checks the change. CD ships it. CT keeps the model fresh."
7

Three levels of maturity

minutes 10–13
Point
where companies are, and where they should aim.
Say it
Level 0, manual: a data scientist hands a model file to engineers; releases are rare; nobody watches it. Level 1, automated pipeline: new data triggers validate → train → evaluate → deploy by itself; the pipeline itself is still installed by hand. Level 2, CI/CD for the pipeline: even changes to the pipeline are tested and deployed automatically. Analogy: Level 0 is cooking every meal by hand; Level 1 is a bread machine that bakes by itself when you add flour; Level 2 is a factory that can also change its own recipe safely. Most companies are at Level 0; aim for Level 1.
Land
"Most companies live at Level 0. The realistic goal is Level 1."

"Where was NorthRetail?" (Level 0.) "And IberBank after the Friday hotfix?" (Level 0, with a laptop.)

The ML test pyramid

minutes 16–19
Point
three layers of tests, cheap and frequent at the bottom, expensive and rare at the top.
Say it
code tests, every push, seconds: does each function do what it says, do the steps fit together? Data tests, every new extract: the right columns, sensible ranges, this week's data, roughly the usual number of rows, nothing that leaks the answer. Model tests, every candidate: a minimum score, not worse than the model in production, fair by segment, fast enough. Analogy: a restaurant kitchen: check the knives work every morning (code), inspect each delivery of ingredients (data), taste each dish before it leaves the kitchen (model). A failure at any layer stops the pipeline.
Land
"Nothing reaches production without passing all three layers."
Ask
"For a fraud model, which layer would catch a supplier sending amounts in cents instead of euros?" (Data tests: ranges.) This is Forum 3, Q1.

The artefact travels in a box: containers, then launch the poll

minutes 31–33
Point
a container packs the model with its exact environment so it runs the same anywhere.
Say it
the shipping container is the analogy: before it, every port loaded cargo its own way; after it, one standard box fits every ship, train and lorry. A software container does the same for a model: the code, the libraries and their exact versions, in one box that runs the same on a laptop or in the cloud. Compared with a virtual machine: a VM carries a whole operating system (a house on a lorry), a container carries only what the app needs (a suitcase), so it starts in seconds. Docker makes the box; Kubernetes runs thousands of them (after the break). Then launch the chat poll and leave it open during the demo on the next slide: "A model that passed all automated tests can be released to 100% of customers at once." AGREE or DISAGREE, one line why; "type it but don't press Enter until I say go". At the end of the demo, read two contrasting answers and do not resolve: it becomes Forum 3, Theme A, the rollback drill. For your Friday synthesis: tests tell you the model is not broken; they cannot tell you how customers, fraudsters or the business will react.

Zero downtime, rollback, and the basic strategy

minutes 35–37
Point
two definitions, and the strategy that ignores them.
Say it
zero downtime: at no moment is the service down or answering wrongly because of the release; customers never notice. For card payments it is not optional: a minute without the model means every payment is either blocked or waved through. Rollback: going back to the previous version (model, code and configuration), which must still exist and be deployable in minutes. Basic deployment: replace everything at once, like changing all the tyres of a car while it is driving: fast and cheap, and for a moment everyone gets the untested version, or nothing. Fine for internal tools, test environments or a Sunday at 4 a.m.
Land
"If you can't roll back in minutes, you don't have a rollback."
19

Blue-green

minutes 37–39
Point
two identical environments and one switch.
Say it
one environment is live with v1 (here called green), a full copy (blue) gets v2 and is tested with no customers; when it is accepted, all traffic is switched over at once, and the old one stays ready as the rollback. Analogy: a theatre with two identical stages: the new set is built behind the curtain, and at the interval the audience is turned to face it; if it fails, they turn back. Pros: simple, rollback is flipping the switch back. Cons: two full production environments cost money, realistic test traffic is hard, keeping both truly identical is hard. (If someone has read that "blue" is usually the live one: the colours are only names; what matters is that one is live and one is waiting.)
Land
"Zero downtime, if the switch is instant; but everyone meets v2 at the same moment."
20

Shadow

minutes 39–41
Point
the new model scores everything and nobody sees its answers.
Say it
every real request is answered by v1; v2 scores the same request in the background and its answer is only written down; after enough requests you compare. Analogy: a driving pupil with a dual-control instructor: the pupil decides what they would do at every junction, but the instructor is the one who actually drives. Pros: v2 sees real data with zero customer risk; rollback is switching it off; proof of equivalence before anyone is affected. Cons: twice the compute; you learn what v2 would have said, not how customers or fraudsters react.
Land
"IberBank's regulator will like this one: proof on real traffic before any customer is affected."
21

Canary

minutes 41–43
Point
a small slice of customers first, then more, then everyone.
Say it
named after the canary in the coal mine: miners took a bird underground because it would react to gas before they did. A small share of traffic (1%, 5%, 10%) goes to v2 and is watched; if healthy, the share grows step by step until v1 is retired. Also like a soft opening of a restaurant for friends and family before the public. Pros: no second full environment, risk bounded by the slice, the lowest-risk way to reach real customers. Cons: two behaviours in production at once; the slice must be big enough to see problems and small enough to limit the damage; you need monitoring good enough to decide (Session 7).
Land
"Shadow exposes nobody; canary exposes a few, on purpose, to learn how real customers react."
22

A/B testing is not a canary

minutes 43–45
Point
same picture (traffic split), different purpose: safe versus better.
Say it
a canary asks "is v2 safe?": it watches errors and speed and grows the slice when nothing breaks. An A/B test asks "is v2 better?": two comparable groups chosen at random, one metric decided in advance, a decision after enough traffic. Analogy: a clinical trial: you decide before the trial what "works" means, and you don't stop the moment the first patient feels better. Three ingredients: a metric (fraud caught at a fixed false-positive rate), a threshold ("v2 wins if it catches 2 points more fraud with the same false positives"), enough traffic (so the difference is not luck; more than a busy afternoon; the statisticians set the number).
Land
"Metric, threshold, enough traffic: decided before you start, or it's not a test."
Ask
"What would be the wrong metric for a fraud model?" (Accuracy: blocking nothing scores 99.6%.) This is Forum 3, Q3.

Scaling

minutes 51–53
Point
a bigger machine, more machines, or both, automatically.
Say it
vertical: a bigger machine, like hiring one faster cashier: simple, but there is a fastest possible cashier, and they're expensive. Horizontal: more machines behind a load balancer, like opening more tills with someone directing customers to the shortest queue: no ceiling, more moving parts. Autoscaling: tills open and close by themselves; scheduled (we know the Black Friday rush is coming) or dynamic (when the queue gets long, open another; when it shrinks, close one).
Land
"Elastic means you pay for the peak only during the peak."
27

Kubernetes in one slide

minutes 53–55
Point
Kubernetes is the manager of the boxes.
Say it
first, 20 seconds of definition, because students have only heard the name once (slide 15): "Kubernetes is software, free and open source, originally from Google, that manages containers across many machines. The name is Greek for 'helmsman', which is why its logo is a ship's wheel; you'll also see it written 'K8s'." (One product: "Kubernetes is".) Then: with hundreds of containers on dozens of machines, someone must start the right ones at the right time, connect them, and replace the ones that fail, day and night. Kubernetes does that automatically. Analogy: an orchestra conductor, or a port's container terminal: Docker builds the containers; Kubernetes decides which ship, which crane and which berth, and reroutes when a crane breaks. Scalable means you can add machines; elastic means the system adds and removes them by itself.
Land
"Docker makes the box; Kubernetes runs thousands of boxes."
28

Serverless

minutes 55–58
Point
pay for the seconds the model runs, and nothing else.
Say it
three ways to host the endpoint: run your own web server (you own the machine and the patches), a cloud ML platform (SageMaker, Vertex AI, Azure ML, watsonx.ai host it for you), or serverless functions: you upload a function and the cloud runs it only when a request arrives. Analogy: a taxi versus owning a car: no parking, no insurance, you pay per trip; but you wait for it to arrive (a "cold start") and, if you travel all day every day, owning is cheaper. Fits: irregular traffic, simple models, no infrastructure team. Does not fit: strict latency, heavy models, constant high traffic: the fraud model.
Land
"Serverless is perfect for the model nobody calls at night, and wrong for the one called 40 million times a month."

Debrief

minutes 76–86
Point
three rooms present, then the strong answer, then the closing line.
Say it
pick three rooms with different plans if you can (one with shadow, one with canary only, one with blue-green). Then walk the table: plan: shadow for two to three weeks (the regulator's proof, inside the four-week budget), then a canary by channel, e-commerce first, 5% → 25% → 100%, with the A/B rule inside; not blue-green alone (no proof on real traffic, everyone switched at once), not basic (zero downtime). A/B rule: confirmed fraud caught per 1,000 flagged, at a false-positive rate no higher than v1's; v2 wins if at least 5% better; at least a full week including a weekend. Rollback trigger: false-positive rate 10% above v1's for an hour, or the review queue above 300 a day, or p95 latency above 150 ms; an automated monitor pages the on-call ML engineer; the canary shrinks to 0%. Who signs: product owner (each go), Risk & Compliance (the regulator's evidence), ML engineer (executes). Correct gently: blue-green alone; a metric with no threshold; "we'll decide the rollback when we see a problem".
Land
"Every strategy is a way to make a mistake small. The pipeline decided the change was safe; the rollout decides how many customers find out if it wasn't." Say it slowly.
