MLOps · Master in Business Analytics and Data Science · MBDS-PT2026F · Video 1 (Session 9), Monitoring Metrics in Depth · 60 minutes, recorded in seven segments

The full word-for-word script stays in the speaker notes if you lose the thread. Timings match the notes.

The shape: opening (1–2) → the drifts (3–7) → performance (8–12) → ground truth (13–15) → operational (16) → LLM deployments (17–20) → alerts in production, with the demo (21–24) → wrap-up (25). Record one segment per take; a mistake costs one segment, not the hour.

Three threads to pull all the way through, and the three things on slide 25: the evidence decides the timing (no truth needed → today; truth needed → late, partial or never), every performance metric is a ratio of four boxes, at a threshold someone chose, and a metric is only a control when it is a rule with a route and an action.

Segment 1 · The drifts

min 3–13½ · slides 3–7
3

Two windows and five choices

3:00–5:30
Point
every drift metric compares two windows; your choices decide if the alert means anything.
Say it
the model is a photo of June; the world keeps moving; drift = distance between photo and world · reference (training data or the first good weeks) vs current window (last week, last month, last 10,000 requests) · too short = noise, too long = late · then the five choices on the slide: reference · window · threshold (from the cost of a false alarm vs a missed one, not a p-value alone) · weighting (drift in a feature the model ignores is noise) · segments (drift hides in a region) · Evidently, the cloud monitors, watsonx.governance compute the same distances.
Land
"The tools compute the distance. These five choices are yours, and they are where monitoring succeeds or fails."
4

Input drift

5:30–8:00
Point
the inputs no longer look like the training inputs.
Say it
the picture: monthly charges, June centred on €62, October on €76, a new pricing plan · numeric: a distance between curves (Wasserstein: how far you would move one pile of sand to make the other) or a test (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, a p-value) · categorical: Jensen–Shannon or chi-squared · two cautions: on big data, tests flag harmless changes, so read the distance · one drifted feature is a question, half of them is an alarm · catches: units, pricing, a new segment · misses: same inputs, new behaviour.
Land
"It needs the inputs only: no labels, no waiting. Remember that for segment three."
5

Prediction drift

8:00–10:00
Point
the model's own outputs moved: the earliest warning you get.
Say it
the drift people forget, and the most useful when labels are late · June: 12% of customers above 50% risk; October: 21% · needs only the logged predictions · why it matters: it summarises all input drifts as the model sees them, plus a hint of concept drift · catches: broken combinations, a deployment that flags everyone or nobody, a growing segment · misses: a model confidently wrong in the same proportions.
Land
"For that you need labels, and that is the third drift."
6

Concept drift

10:00–12:00
Point
same inputs, different outcome.
Say it
Session 7's demo: a competitor launched fibre in the North; long-tenure customers who never churned started leaving · tenure didn't move, region didn't move; behaviour moved · drift report: nothing; accuracy in that segment 89% → 65% · you only know with labels, by segment, over time · you guess early with prediction drift, a shift in which features drive explanations, and the business KPI ("the call list feels wrong") · four shapes: sudden, gradual, seasonal (not drift if the model knows the season), recurring.
Land
"It catches everything the other two miss. What it costs is waiting for the truth."
7

The three drifts side by side

12:00–13:30
Point
the summary table for the quiz.
Say it
read across, one line each: input = the features moved, inputs only, daily · prediction = the outputs moved, predictions only, daily, the earliest warning · concept = the relationship moved, needs labels, monthly or later.
Land
"The first two buy time while you wait for the third."

Segment 2 · Performance

min 13½–24 · slides 8–12
8

The confusion matrix

13:30–15:30
Point
one picture behind every performance metric.
Say it
1,000 customers scored in June · model flagged 250 · 200 actually churned · cross them: 140 flagged and left (true positives) · 110 flagged but stayed (false positives, called for nothing) · 60 left unflagged (false negatives, missed) · 690 stayed, not flagged (true negatives) · every metric in this segment is a ratio of these four.
Land
"The rows are the truth. Without knowing who really churned, you cannot fill a single box. Hold that."
9

Accuracy

15:30–17:00
Point
how often the model is right, and why that can lie.
Say it
(140 + 690) / 1,000 = 83% · fine for balanced problems; churn at 20% is usable, with care · the fraud model: 10,000 transactions, 40 fraud; a model that blocks nothing scores 99.6% and catches zero · accuracy rewards the majority.
Land
"When someone says '99% accurate', ask first: how rare is the thing it predicts?"
10

Precision, recall and the threshold

17:00–20:00
Point
two metrics that trade against each other through one dial.
Say it
precision: of what was flagged, how much was right · 140 / 250 = 56% · low precision = wasted calls, customers wrongly blocked · recall: of what really happened, how much was caught · 140 / 200 = 70% · low recall = churners never called, fraud that went through · retention: precision is money (each call €4); fraud: recall is money, precision is reputation · the model says 0.73, someone decides that above 0.5 means churn · threshold 0.3: 400 flagged, recall 85%, precision 42% · 0.7: 120 flagged, precision 75%, recall 45% · María chose 0.4 by looking at the test set; a retention manager should choose it from the budget · F1 compares models, it does not explain them.
Land
"The threshold is a business setting, not a model property. Report the metrics at the threshold you actually use."
11

AUC and calibration

20:00–22:30
Point
"does it still rank?" versus "can I trust the number?".
Say it
AUC without the curve: pick one churner and one stayer at random; AUC = probability the churner got the higher score · 0.5 coin toss, 0.8 good for churn, 1.0 perfect; the churn model in the Session 3 demo scored about 0.87 · good for comparing versions, says nothing about your threshold · calibration: take everyone scored 75–85%; did about 80% churn? if only 50% did, the model is overconfident · people act on the number: a €50 discount to everyone "above 80%" wastes half the budget if 80% means 50% · curve bucket by bucket; one number, the Brier score; by segment too.
Land
"Drift often breaks calibration before it breaks ranking: the order is still right, every score is too high. And both need the truth."
12

Which metric for which decision

22:30–24:00
Point
the decision picks the metric.
Say it
read the left column as questions: balanced, right overall? accuracy · wasting effort on false alarms? precision · missing the cases that matter? recall · which model ranks better? AUC · can we trust the number to price an offer? calibration · always by segment, because the overall number hides the North · two habits: write the threshold next to the metric; store every prediction with model version and date, or the truth cannot be joined back.
Land
"Look at the last column: every one of these needs the truth. Which brings us to the segment most courses skip."

Segment 3 · Ground truth

min 24–31 · slides 13–15
13

Which metrics need the truth

24:00–26:00
Point
half the metrics in this video wait for the world to answer.
Say it
ground truth = what really happened · in training it was in the dataset; in production it arrives from the world: slow, partial, sometimes silent · no truth needed: input and prediction drift, operational metrics (that is why they are early warnings) · truth needed: every performance metric, concept drift, fairness · LLM quality: the truth is a judgement, only for the sample someone judged · when it arrives: churn 30 days, from billing, for everyone (the easy case) · fraud: chargebacks 30–90 days, only if the customer complains · credit default 12 months, only for those approved · forecast: end of period, only for what was in stock.
Land
"An honest monitoring plan has a column that says when the truth arrives, and for how many cases."
Pause
"Pause and note which metrics need the truth."
14

Four ways the truth goes missing

26:00–29:00
Point
late, decoupled, partial, absent; the third is the trap.
Say it
late: October's churn chart can only be drawn in November, credit a year later; meanwhile the dashboard should say "pending", not draw a flat line · decoupled: predictions keyed by transaction, chargebacks by card and date; nobody designed the join, so only half match, and not a random half · partial: IberBank blocks a payment, you never learn if it was fraud, because it never happened · a rejected loan never defaults · 100 units stocked, 100 sold, demand was maybe 150 · selective labels: truth only for the cases the model let through · absent: the HR assistant answers, the employee closes the window; the only truth is a judgement made later, for a sample.
Land
"A model that blocks, rejects or limits is grading its own homework on the cases it chose to let through."
15

Five practices for living without the truth

29:00–31:00
Point
what you actually do about it; none is free.
Say it
one, proxies: prediction drift, the business KPI, blocked customers who call to complain, thumbs down; they trigger investigation, never retraining on their own · two, design the join before go-live: id, timestamp, model version, an agreed key to the outcome · three, backfill and recompute: when labels land, fill October's slot, by segment · four, random samples let through: IberBank lets one in two hundred flagged payments through; the only honest measure of what the model hides; a cost, and a fairness question (whom does the model never learn about?) · five, deliberate labelling: pay for truth, e.g. 100 assistant conversations reviewed a week, 50 rejected loans audited a month.
Land
"All five are cheaper than learning a month late that the model was wrong, or never learning at all."

End of segment three.

Segment 4 · Operational

16

The machine's side

31:00–34:00
Point
the metrics IT already collects, and why the average lies.
Say it
latency: one prediction's time · throughput: predictions per second · error rate: requests that fail · none needs the truth, all are real-time · the bank queue: average wait 4 minutes, but one in twenty waits 25; the percentile shows them, the average hides them · p95 = the time within which 95% are answered · "under 150 ms" must mean p95 under 150 ms · model additions: missing or out-of-range inputs (the Session 3 checks, live) · batch on time: the churn list before the team starts at 06:00; a late batch is an outage · cost per 1,000 predictions next to accuracy · freshness: NorthRetail ran four months (February to June) on empty features.
Land
"When you see a latency number, ask: which percentile?"

End of segment four.

Segment 5 · LLM deployments

min 34–45 · slides 17–20
17

What is different with a language model

34:00–36:30
Point
four differences that change the metrics.
Say it
new running example: an internal HR assistant answering from policy documents (a RAG system; Session 10 builds it) · one: usually no single right answer, so quality is judged, and then we measure the judge; segment three's "absent truth" is the normal case here · two: the model is rarely yours; what drifts is the prompt, the documents and the questions · three: cost is per token; a verbose answer or a pasted document costs more · four: new failures: confident falsehoods, offensive output, leaked personal data.
Land
"Human feedback matters more here than anywhere: it is often the only label you will ever get."
18

Judge, then measure the judge

36:30–39:00
Point
three ways to judge quality, and where each fits.
Say it
reference-based (ROUGE, BLEU, similarity): compare with an approved answer; cheap, but needs a reference, so it runs on a golden set of questions, on every change to prompt, model or documents · judge-based: another model scores the answer against a rubric (correct, complete, faithful, 1–5), given the question and the retrieved passages; scales to live traffic; check it against humans, because judges prefer long answers · human review: the gold standard, expensive; e.g. 100 conversations a week by the HR team, same rubric as the judge.
Land
"Golden set on every change, judge daily, humans weekly: deliberate labelling made routine."
19

The failures you count on every request

39:00–41:30
Point
hallucination, toxicity, personal data, and losing the ground.
Say it
hallucination rate: answers not supported by the sources; a judge checks each claim, humans confirm a sample; an invented number of holiday days has legal consequences; in the Air Canada case (Session 10) a tribunal made the airline pay for what its chatbot had invented · toxicity (HAP): hate, abuse, profanity, in both directions · PII: names, identifiers, salaries; any PII in an output is an incident, not a trend · retrieval systems add two halves: retrieval quality (was the right passage found? hit rate on the golden set) and groundedness (was the answer built from it? correct but ungrounded is still a failure).
Land
"After a policy update, groundedness tells you whether the assistant answers from the new policy or from memory."
20

Tokens, feedback and the three drifts

41:30–45:00
Point
the operational side, the label you can get, and what drifts.
Say it
latency in two parts: time to first token and total time, p95 of both · tokens in (question + passages + instructions) and out; the passages usually dominate, so a retrieval change can double the cost · cost = tokens × price, with a budget line; cost drift is real · feedback: thumbs up/down (biased, still the best daily signal), escalation and rephrasing, resolution rate, the weekly human review · three drifts: the questions (new topics), the documents (a policy update), the answers (longer, more refusals) · store every conversation with prompt, document-set and model versions.
Land
"Vendor dashboards put all of this on one screen. Different vendors, same families. Next: what happens when a number crosses a line."

End of segment five.

An alert is a rule

45:00–47:30
Point
a monitoring plan is a short list of rules, not twenty charts.
Say it
six parts: metric · threshold · window · severity (wake someone at 3 a.m., or post to a channel?) · route (who receives it) · action (what they do) · on screen, alerts.yaml, eight rules for TelcoNova churn, in the demo folder · read drifted-features: how many of the model's six inputs drifted vs June; two or more fires; weekly; trend; Slack channel and ML engineer; check the extract, retrain if confirmed · missing-features: tenure missing for more than 5%; incident; page on-call; hold the call list (the NorthRetail bug, as a rule) · the performance rules say "monthly, when labels arrive": segment three, written into the file · the accuracy rule exists twice, overall and by segment, with a minimum size so twelve customers cannot page anyone.
Land
"A rule without a route and an action is a chart, not a control."
