### Q1 · Why models never reach production

> Thank you all: 27 of you answered this one, and most with real cases, which is exactly what I hoped for. A few things stood out.
>
> **The best answers drew reasons from different layers.** Marcos (compliance, legacy integration, tied-agent sales), Camila Gómez (serving, use, peak capacity) and Anas (signal, regulation, scale) each covered three different families. Each also said why *that* persona and not another. Compare that with answers where the data scientist, or the product owner, appears in two of the three reasons.
>
> **The sharpest line of the week** came from Khem, on Aldi Nord: *"the platform can be 100% up while every order proposal is wrong."* That is the difference between IT/DevOps and ML engineering in one sentence. Armen said the same from the other side: *"green means up, not right."*
>
> **Two debates worth reading.** First, is a problem a *reason a model never reaches production*, or just *the work of getting it there*? (Khem to Marcos.) A reason only one persona can unblock is a real blocker; a task with a known owner is a plan. Second, Khem and Barbara both separated *"is the data secure?"* from *"are we allowed to use it?"*. A secure pipeline answers the first question and not the second. That decision sits with Risk & Compliance, and the Data Engineer implements it.
>
> **The persona most often missing** was the business owner of the decision the model feeds: Edgar's supply-chain lead, Sigursteinn's pricing owner, Mohamed's sales rep. A model nobody acts on never reaches production in any meaningful sense.

### Q2 · A responsibility map

> 20 maps, and no two the same. That's fine, because I'm grading the reasoning. Three patterns:
>
> **1. One A per row is the point of the exercise.** Several maps had rows with no A at all, or merged stages so that validation disappeared inside "model development". When a row has no A, nobody answers when that stage fails. That is how a model dies, as Armen wrote about his elasticity model: *"nobody held the A."*
>
> **2. Who signs off validation?** You split into two camps. Ayushi, Fabio, Ana Cristina and Khem gave the A to the Product Owner ("good enough" is a business call). Marcos, Armen and Sigursteinn gave it to Risk & Compliance, because the model recommends financial or insurance products, and *"the business should not mark its own homework."* Both camps are right in their own context. Khem's note says when to switch: *if a recommendation affects someone's access to credit, a job or a price, the A moves to Risk.*
>
> **3. Monitoring: R and A are different questions.** The ML Engineer does the work, but Barbara asked who answers *when sales drop*. Fernando split it in two: IT/DevOps watches the system, while the ML Engineer and PO watch the model.
>
> **The open question I want answered before Saturday** comes from Julio M., and it is the best one in the thread: when the model is retrained after drift, does the new version go back through validation? If it doesn't, the ML Engineer is approving a new model alone.

### Q3 · Training vs operationalising

> Most of you defined the difference well. The strongest definitions were short: *"a trained model is a result, an operational model is a service"* (Marcos), *"an experiment that ends vs a service that never ends"* (Armen), *"a task that ends vs work that never ends"* (Khem).
>
> Where answers differed was the **consequence**. The best ones named a mechanism and a cost:
>
> - **Marcos:** claims history refreshed monthly in production instead of in real time, so recent claimants were priced as clean and renewals were underpriced for months.
> - **Ayushi:** a min/max capability that was bought but never switched on, leaving cash on the shelves or an aircraft on the ground.
> - **Sigursteinn:** a spring forecast nobody refreshed, so idle cars kept adding depreciation into the autumn.
> - **Armen:** a model with nowhere to land, producing zero revenue and a shelf life running out.
>
> Weaker answers stopped at "the model will be wrong". Ask yourself: wrong how, noticed by whom, and costing what?
>
> One insight deserves to be singled out. Ana Cristina noticed that if agents only call the leads the model ranks high, the next training set only contains those leads, and the model becomes more biased with every retrain. Tanvi and Sigursteinn replied with the fix: a small random holdout. Tanvi also saw the trade-off. It costs calls today, so it is the Product Owner's decision, not the data scientist's. That is a feedback loop, and we will come back to it in the monitoring session.

### Theme A · The three-model company

> No one argued that the *number* of models sets the threshold, and that is the right conclusion. Armen put it best: *"one model that prices loans every day needs more operations than ten models that feed a quarterly slide."* Fernando gave the threshold in one line: *the cost of having a model unstable.* The cost of MLOps scales with the cost of being wrong.
>
> Barbara and Armen both described the cheapest version, and they agree: a person's name next to each model, a tagged version you can roll back to, one business number checked on a schedule, and a written retraining trigger. Barbara added the counter-intuitive part. A company *without* a data culture needs this *more*, because it would take longer to notice a failure.

### Theme B · Two job postings

> Armen and Gerard saw through both titles. Posting A is mostly a Data Engineer, with one line of real ML engineering ("turn prototypes into production-ready code"). Posting B is three or four personas in one chair: the hand-over problem "solved" by having one person hand over to themselves, which works until that person leaves.
>
> What's missing from both: everything after deployment (monitoring, retraining, an owner once the model is live), and Risk & Compliance. Armen spotted the sharpest point. B builds pricing and claims models for an insurer that sells *health* insurance. Under the EU AI Act, risk assessment and pricing of individuals in life and health insurance is high-risk (Annex III). Yet the same person who builds the model would validate it. (Motor pricing is not on that list, a nuance worth knowing.)
>
> Open question for anyone still reading: in a company that hires from these postings, who does the monitoring and the compliance work? Very often the answer is nobody, until the audit.

### Friday synthesis

> **What you said.** Three ideas from this week are worth keeping.
>
> - Khem and Armen: *a service can be up and still wrong.* Infrastructure health is not model health.
> - Marcos and Khem: decide who signs off validation by the risk of the decision. Where a recommendation touches credit, insurance or price, the A moves to Risk & Compliance.
> - Ana Cristina, Tanvi and Sigursteinn: when a model decides who gets acted on, it also decides what it will learn from next. A small random holdout is the price of keeping it honest, and that is a business decision.
>
> **The misconception of the week.** Many answers treated operationalising as "putting the model on a server". A server is necessary but not sufficient. Operationalising means the model answers real requests every day with today's data, its output lands where a decision is made, and a named person is accountable when it is wrong. Several of your own stories (Armen's pricing engine, Ayushi's min/max, Fabio's care teams) were models that worked and were never used.
>

>

## How the questions worked

The three questions did their job: they produced real cases and real disagreement. Theme B and the Thursday twist barely surfaced, so here are three things to adjust next time.

- **Q1 worked best.** The phrase "why that persona and not another" drove the quality. Keep it, and add "from three different layers" to the prompt, since about a third of answers drew two reasons from the same family.
- **Q2 needs the template enforced.** About half the maps collapsed the 10 template rows into 4–6 stages, which hid the validation sign-off. Several maps had rows with no A. Ask for "all 10 rows of the template, exactly one A per row" in bold, and offer a pasteable table, since two image uploads were cut off in the export.
- **Q3 needs "with a cost".** Definitions were good across the board, but around a third of the consequences had no mechanism or cost. Add "name who noticed, when, and what it cost" to the prompt.
- **Theme B got 2 answers** (Armen and Gerard), and no Theme C thread or reply to the Q2 regulation twist appears in the export. Theme B arrived on Tuesday, when most students had already posted, so it read as optional. Consider posting Theme B with the opening message, or making one theme reply part of the grade.
- **Timing.** About a third of the posts arrived on Thursday or Friday, so several good challenges (to Marcos, Armen and Julio F.) went unanswered. A stated rule such as "answers by Tuesday, replies by Thursday" would give the debate room to happen.
